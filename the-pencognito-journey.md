The cameo - But fortunately, we had our savior, actually in our side pocket. The ESP32-cam module. though we had to change the whole system architecture just to make this new guy do the same work.

It started simple enough: an ESP32-CAM, two L298N motor drivers, and an idea to build a little rover I could drive around from my laptop while watching its point of view through a live video feed. Nothing fancy at first — just wire the motors up, get the camera streaming, and slap together a web page with some directional buttons so I could poke at it from my phone if I wanted to.

The ESP32 side came together as two separate little web servers living on the same board: one on port 80 handling plain HTTP routes like `/forward`, `/left`, `/stop` — dead simple, just flip some GPIO pins high or low depending on which route got hit — and a second one on port 81 whose only job was to keep pumping out an MJPEG stream from the camera. Two ports because the ESP32's HTTP server library gets cranky trying to do continuous video streaming and quick request/response control on the same handler at the same time, so splitting them kept everything responsive.

Once that was flashed and the board was pinned to a static IP on my WiFi so it wouldn't wander off to a new address every reboot, I moved the real brains over to my laptop. That's where `laptop_brain.py` came in — pulling the video stream over WiFi, decoding the MJPEG frames, and giving me a proper window to watch the world through the bot's eyes. I threw YOLOv8 on top just so I could see object labels floating over the video for fun, even though the bot doesn't actually act on any of that — it's purely eye candy while I drive.

The actual driving started out manual: hold W to go forward, A and D to turn, S to reverse, space bar to stop. Except my first pass at this used OpenCV's key events, which only fire on key-down — there's no clean "key was released" signal in that world. So the bot would just... keep going after I let go of the key, however OpenCV had queued things up, until the next explicit stop. Not great for something with wheels. The fix was to stop relying on OpenCV for input entirely and reach into Windows directly with `GetAsyncKeyState`, which tells you the true, live, physically-held-right-now state of any key on the keyboard. That actually introduced a new problem — because `GetAsyncKeyState` doesn't care what window is focused, it just reports the raw keyboard state system-wide. Which meant if I clicked away to browse something else while a key happened to still be resting funny, the bot could start driving without me even looking at it. So a focus check got bolted on: the bot only ever acts on key state while its own window is the one actually in the foreground. Click away, and driving stops dead and stays stopped until you click back.

Then came the speed problem. The ESP32 firmware never got a proper PWM speed API — it only knows full-send forward, full-send left, full-send right, full-send reverse, or stop. No in-between. Driving it at "full send" only made the thing basically uncontrollable, spinning past where I wanted it to go every time. Since there was no real speed knob to turn, I ended up faking one: instead of holding a drive command on continuously, the laptop pulses it on and off rapidly — a software duty cycle. Tune how much of each cycle is "on" versus "off," and you get an effective slower crawl out of a motor that only knows how to be either fully on or fully off.

Manual driving solved, the fun part was next: making it chase things on its own. I picked plain color detection for this rather than anything YOLO-based — specifically, hunting for a red blob in the HSV color space, since red is easy to isolate and doesn't need a trained model. Press P, and the bot switches into "chase the red object" mode. Lose the target, and it stops rather than blindly driving off into a wall or off a desk. Any manual key press instantly yanks control back and drops it out of autonomous mode — no fighting the bot for the keyboard.

Getting the *steering* of that chase mode to feel right took a few passes. The naive version was just "if the blob is left of center, spin left; if it's right of center, spin right" — which technically works but looks and drives terribly, all stop-turn-stop-go, way overreacting to every little wobble in the blob's position. So I built it out properly with a PID controller running on the normalized horizontal offset of the target from the center of frame — the P term reacting to how far off it currently is, the D term damping the overshoot by watching how fast that error is changing, and I left the I term basically unused since there wasn't a persistent steering bias worth correcting for. On top of the PID output, the logic branches into two behaviors depending on how bad the misalignment is: if the target's way off to one side, it does a quick rotate-in-place burst; if it's only moderately off, it does a much gentler "arc drive" — a tiny turn nudge immediately followed by continuing forward, so it's correcting its aim *while* still closing the distance instead of stopping dead to reorient every single frame. As it gets closer — measured by how much of the frame width the target's bounding box fills — the forward pulses ease off so it doesn't come in too hot and overshoot the thing it's chasing.

WiFi lag turned out to be its own beast. Every command takes a moment to actually reach the bot and take effect, and the bot itself takes a moment to physically stop once told to. Left unaddressed, the loop would keep reading "stale" frames and making decisions based on where the target *was*, not where it currently is, which caused a lot of overcorrecting and wobble. The fix was a two-phase timing loop: send a pulse, wait for it to finish, then explicitly send a stop and hold for a short settle period before even looking at the next frame to make a new decision — giving both the physical hardware and the network time to actually catch up with what was commanded.

Then there was the infamous mirrored-steering bug — the one where holding a red pen out to the right made the bot spin left instead of turning to face it, like it was steering in a mirror. Manual driving never touched the video frame at all, so it couldn't have been the cause there — which pointed straight at the camera feed itself being mirrored relative to how the chassis physically turns, a pretty common quirk depending on how these little camera modules get mounted. An `INVERT_STEERING` flag flipped the sign on the error term and cleared it right up.

And then, because a chasing robot alone wasn't quite enough, I added a second motor — wired to its own L298N — nicknamed the "slash" motor. Once the bot closes the distance and the target fills enough of the frame to count as "arrived," it stops and fires this second motor in a single burst. There's a little bit of bookkeeping so it doesn't just machine-gun that motor over and over while sitting parked on the target — it only re-arms itself once the target either gets lost or backs off well clear of the "arrived" distance again. There's also a manual test-fire key bound in for bench-testing the slash motor by itself without needing to run the whole autonomous chase.

Which is how this thing ended up, in its final form, as a small WiFi rover that hunts down anything red in its camera's view, closes in on it with genuinely tuned PID steering instead of just lurching around, and takes a swing at it with a second motor when it arrives — which is exactly the kind of unhinged desk-side chaos gremlin that "sneaks up and messes with whatever red object catches its eye on your desk" energy that makes it fun rather than just another remote-control car.

The most recent chapter was just getting the laptop and the board talking to each other reliably again — a timeout that turned out to be the laptop script pointed at the wrong port for the video stream (it needed to be 81, not 80, since that's where the stream server actually lives on the ESP32 side), and then a 404 after that fix, which usually means the board answered but the currently-running firmware didn't actually have the route being asked for — worth double-checking with a fresh reflash and a look at the Serial Monitor on boot to confirm the camera initialized properly and the board actually got to the point of registering its handlers.
